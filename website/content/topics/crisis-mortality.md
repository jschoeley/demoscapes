# Crisis mortality

Mortality crises such as wars, epidemics and famines are sudden, short-lived shocks to the risk of dying. On a Lexis surface of death rates they stand out as *period effects*: narrow vertical bands in which mortality is far higher than in the years just before and after. But a crisis rarely hits all ages equally, and the Lexis surface shows not only *when* it struck but also *whom* it struck. The death rates of men in England & Wales since the mid 19th century (HMD; see below) make this visible. During the First (1914–1918) and Second World War (1939–1945), dark bands appear that are largely confined to ages of roughly 18 to 40, the ages of men in active military service, while children and the elderly were barely affected. Immediately after the First World War, the influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 left a second mark. Unlike most influenza seasons, which mainly kill infants and the elderly, this pandemic was unusually deadly for young adults aged 20 to 40 (Taubenberger2006). Because it struck women as well as men, comparing the male surface with the female one helps to separate the losses of war from those of the epidemic.
